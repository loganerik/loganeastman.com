IMBY: In My Back Yard

An interactive exploration of how regulations shaped Los Angeles—and what reform could unlock.

🕐 Timeline Slider

Drag the slider to travel through time (1900–2026). Watch as regulations accumulate and neighborhoods freeze. The map colors show R1 (single-family only) zoning density.

🔧 Reform Toggles

In the left panel, switch to the Reform tab to experiment with policy changes. Toggle reforms on/off to see their impact on potential housing units, GDP, construction costs, and approval timelines.

📊 Methodology

Housing impact estimates use the Hsieh-Moretti model (2019) linking housing constraints to GDP. Zoning data comes from LA City Planning (ZIMAS) and LA County Assessor records. Historical transit routes are from Pacific Electric Railway Historical Society and Metro archives.

This is a research tool, not a policy prescription. All estimates are directional—actual impacts depend on market conditions, implementation, and countless other factors.

Sources & Methodology

Economic Model: Hsieh & Moretti (2019), "Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation," American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics. Found housing constraints reduced US GDP growth by ~36% from 1964-2009. (Note: The authors later acknowledged arithmetic errors, though the directional finding holds.)

Calculation: Additional units × 2.3 (household size) × 1.4 (workers/household) × wage premium (20%) × agglomeration multiplier (1.15)

Zoning Data: LA City Planning (ZIMAS), LA County Assessor, Census Bureau ZCTA boundaries

Key Sources:

  • Glaeser & Gyourko (2018), "The Economic Implications of Housing Supply," Journal of Economic Perspectives
  • Saiz (2010), "The Geographic Determinants of Housing Supply," Quarterly Journal of Economics
  • Lens & Monkkonen (2016), "Do Strict Land Use Regulations Make Metropolitan Areas More Segregated by Income?"
  • Manville et al. (2020), "Zoning and Affordability: A Reply to Critics," UCLA Lewis Center
  • Diamond, McQuade & Qian (2019), "The Effects of Rent Control Expansion on Tenants, Landlords, and Inequality," American Economic Review

Rent Control: Stanford study found SF rent control reduced rental supply by 15% and increased citywide rents by 5.1%. Benefits accrue disproportionately to longer-term tenants regardless of income.

Historical: Center for Pacific Urbanism (1958 zoning), LA Public Library (Sanborn maps, Baist Atlas)
